# Vision 2030 Saudi Arabia: Which Sectors Foreign Companies Can Actually Access and How

![Vision 2030 Saudi Arabia Strategic Initiative](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1552664730-d307ca884978?w=800)

Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 represents the largest infrastructure and diversification programme in modern history. Announced in April 2016 by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, the initiative aims to reduce the Kingdom's dependency on oil, diversify the economy, and build a knowledge-based society. But beyond the headline announcements about NEOM, The Red Sea Project, and mega-cities lies a critical question that foreign companies rarely get answered: which of the 96 strategic objectives actually generate near-term procurement opportunities, and what does it take to win?

Most foreign companies approaching Vision 2030 face a fragmented landscape. Generic information abounds about the Kingdom's $900 billion investment pipeline, but actionable intelligence on which sectors are genuinely accessible to international firms, how In-Country Value (ICV) scoring affects contract awards, and what procurement timelines actually look like remains scarce.

This guide cuts through the ambiguity. It maps which Vision 2030 strategic objectives create immediate commercial opportunities, explains the mechanics of ICV scoring and how it shapes foreign company participation, identifies sectors with the fastest market entry paths, and provides realistic procurement timelines based on current project data.

## Understanding Vision 2030 structure and the 96 strategic objectives

Vision 2030 is structured around three pillars: A Vibrant Society, A Thriving Economy, and An Ambitious Nation. Within these pillars sit 96 strategic objectives that span 16 economic sectors.

Not all 96 objectives create equal procurement opportunity. Some are social programmes with limited vendor participation. Others are regulatory or efficiency initiatives unlikely to generate hardware or services contracts. The strategically important distinction is identifying which objectives map directly to capital expenditure, infrastructure projects, or regulated sector licenses.

The highest-value procurement objectives cluster in four areas: infrastructure and logistics, renewable energy and industrial transition, healthcare and life sciences, and technology and digitalisation. Each of these generates recurring, multi-year procurement cycles aligned with Vision 2030 investment timelines that extend through 2030 and beyond.

The remaining objectives either support these four core areas indirectly, focus on workforce development or regulatory modernisation, or remain in pre-implementation phases. Understanding this hierarchy prevents foreign companies from pursuing objectives with no immediate procurement pathway.

## The procurement landscape: How the 96 objectives translate to contracts

![Procurement Supply Chain Network](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1552664730-d307ca884978?w=800)

Roughly 30 of Vision 2030's 96 objectives have active, government-backed procurement pipelines. These fall into two categories: megaprojects with dedicated funding vehicles, and sector-wide digitalisation or infrastructure programmes administered across multiple government entities.

![NEOM Smart City Development](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1504384308090-c894fdcc538d?w=800)

NEOM serves as the clearest example of a megaproject-driven objective. The $500 billion giga-project (now $1.5 trillion with expansions) operates under special procurement rules, draws talent from a global contractor registry, and awarded construction contracts valued at hundreds of millions annually through 2025. The project created dedicated procurement pipelines in construction materials, logistics, desalination, renewable energy, and AI infrastructure.

In contrast, the healthcare modernisation objective does not operate as a single procurement programme. Instead, it disperses across multiple government entities and Public Investment Fund (PIF) investments: the Ministry of Health, specialized clinic networks, pharmaceutical manufacturing initiatives, and biotech research hubs each run independent procurement cycles. Foreign companies participate by understanding which entity controls which subsector and building relationships at that level.

Renewable energy procurement follows a hybrid model. The Kingdom targets up to 130 GW of renewable capacity by 2030. This mandate generates IPP (Independent Power Producer) opportunities, EPC (engineering, procurement, and construction) contracts, technology partnerships, and operations and maintenance services. However, participation typically requires pre-qualification with utilities like Saudi Aramco or ACWA Power, or joint ventures with established Saudi operators.

The most accessible near-term opportunities exist in sectors with centralised procurement and government urgency: logistics infrastructure, green hydrogen, desalination, AI and data centres, and tourism infrastructure. In these sectors, the government procurement timeline often compresses to 18–24 months from initial tender to contract signature.

## The role of In-Country Value (ICV) scoring in foreign company participation

![ICV Scoring Evaluation Process](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1554224311-beee415c15c7?w=800)

In-Country Value scoring is the critical mechanism that determines whether a foreign company can actually win Vision 2030 contracts and at what competitive disadvantage.

Introduced by Saudi Aramco in 2015 as the In-Kingdom Total Value Add (IKTVA) program, ICV has evolved into a mandatory scoring criterion across government procurement, most notably in Vision 2030 contracts. Unlike regulatory local content requirements that impose hard thresholds, ICV operates as a scoring mechanism that affects contract awards even when bids come from fully compliant competitors.

For foreign companies, understanding ICV scoring is non-negotiable. The score measures the percentage of economic value that a company retains or generates within Saudi Arabia across five dimensions: localized goods and services, compensation paid to Saudi nationals, training and development of Saudi employees, supplier development within the Kingdom, and local research and development investment. Export revenue is factored in separately as a percentage-based adjustment.

ICV scoring works like this: a company with an ICV score of 40% and a bid price of 100 million SAR competes against a Saudi-based company with an ICV score of 70% and a bid price of 102 million SAR. Even though the foreign company's raw price is lower, the ICV-weighted evaluation might favour the Saudi company. The exact weighting varies by contract and government entity, but typically ranges from 30% to 50% of the total evaluation score in Vision 2030 projects.

Building a higher ICV score requires foreign companies to establish local presence: invest in Saudi suppliers, establish research or development partnerships with Saudi universities or industrial zones, hire and train Saudi nationals, or commit to manufacturing or services delivery within the Kingdom. For large infrastructure projects, foreign companies often structure deals as joint ventures with Saudi partners, automatically increasing the combined ICV score.

The most successful foreign companies in Vision 2030 procurement treat ICV not as a compliance checkbox but as a business investment. A technology services company targeting NEOM's AI data centre projects might establish a local office, hire Saudi software engineers, and partner with a Saudi cloud infrastructure firm. This approach increases the ICV score from 15% to 50%, making the company competitive against Saudi bidders.

ICV scores are not static. Once a company enrolls in the IKTVA program or participates in government contracts, it develops a five-year action plan to improve the score. Companies that demonstrate consistent ICV improvement often receive preferential treatment in subsequent procurement rounds.

## Sector-by-sector breakdown of Vision 2030 procurement opportunities for foreign companies

### Renewable energy and green hydrogen

![Solar and Wind Renewable Energy Installation](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1509391366360-2e938aa1df86?w=800)

Saudi Arabia targets 130 GW of renewable capacity by 2030, including solar, wind, and green hydrogen. Renewable energy represents one of the most accessible Vision 2030 sectors for foreign companies because the Kingdom lacks domestic manufacturing capacity for solar panels, wind turbines, and electrolyser equipment, and has limited existing expertise in large-scale renewable development.

Foreign companies can participate across the entire value chain. EPC contractors can bid on construction contracts for solar parks and wind farms. Technology providers can supply turbines, inverters, energy storage systems, and digitalisation platforms. Engineering firms can provide design and feasibility studies. Operations and maintenance (O&M) service providers can secure long-term contracts managing completed facilities.

The most mature procurement pipeline is in solar and wind farm development. Saudi Aramco operates independent power producer (IPP) programmes that auction concessions for renewable projects. These projects are structured as long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) with 20–25-year terms, making them attractive for institutional investors and international project developers.

Green hydrogen represents a frontier sector with accelerated procurement. NEOM's green hydrogen facility, a joint venture between NEOM, Air Products, and ACWA Power valued at $8.4 billion, reported 80% construction completion as of early 2025. The project requires electrolyser technology, offshore wind integration, and hydrogen export infrastructure. International electrolyser manufacturers have secured significant contracts here. The facility's operational phase (beginning 2025–2026) will create ongoing procurement for hydrogen end-users, industrial offtake contracts, and export logistics services.

Foreign companies entering renewable energy should expect 18–24 month sales cycles from tender to contract, and require either pre-qualification with major Saudi utilities or a local partner. ICV requirements typically range from 35% to 50%, often satisfied through local operations, Saudi employee hiring, and supply chain localisation agreements with equipment manufacturers.

### Construction, materials and logistics

![Construction Infrastructure Development](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1504917595217-340fc7408808?w=800)

Vision 2030 megaprojects collectively represent 200+ infrastructure initiatives. NEOM alone requires materials, equipment, and services procurement valued at billions annually through 2030. The Red Sea Project, Diriyah, AlUla, Qiddiya, and dozens of infrastructure modernisation programmes require everything from structural steel and concrete to project management software and heavy equipment rental.

Construction materials provide the highest-volume procurement opportunities. Saudi Arabia imports most steel, aluminium, cement speciality products, and electrical systems. Foreign suppliers of construction materials can access procurement through several channels: direct contracts with project owners (NEOM, PIF-backed projects), contracts with general contractors already in the Kingdom, or established distributor networks with pre-qualified supplier status.

Heavy equipment rental and services represent another accessible sector. Foreign equipment companies (crane operators, concrete pump service providers, surveying technology providers) can establish regional headquarters in Saudi Arabia and service multiple Vision 2030 projects. Logistics and supply chain services are also in high demand: cold chain providers, customs brokerage, last-mile delivery for project materials, and warehousing all generate steady procurement.

The logistics sector benefits from particular tailwinds. Saudi Arabia's geographic position as a hub connecting three continents, combined with Vision 2030 investment in ports (Yanbu, Ras Al Khair), airports, and rail infrastructure, creates long-term demand for logistics service providers, software vendors, and supply chain technology companies.

Construction sector procurement is characterised by quick decision cycles and high volumes, but also intense local competition and ICV pressure. Foreign companies typically require a local joint venture partner or established subsidiary to be competitive. ICV scores in construction typically range from 25% to 45%, reflecting the fact that raw materials and equipment have significant imported components.

### Desalination and water infrastructure

![Water Treatment Desalination Facility](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1576091160550-112173782c51?w=800)

Saudi Arabia targets massive expansion of water production to support population growth and industrial development. The Kingdom is accelerating desalination procurement across multiple programmes: the Ministry of Environment, Water and Agriculture runs a capital programme, NEOM is building proprietary zero liquid discharge (ZLD) desalination plants, and industrial clusters require reliable water supplies.

Desalination projects are particularly attractive for foreign companies because they require advanced technology procurement. Reverse osmosis membranes, energy recovery devices, pre-treatment systems, and automated monitoring are typically sourced from international suppliers. EPC contracts for desalination plant construction often go to consortiums of international and local firms.

NEOM's desalination programme illustrates the opportunity. The project is tendering multiple desalination packages valued at $1.5 billion to $2 billion, powered by 100% renewable energy. These projects have 12-month construction cycles and active procurement processes. Foreign companies can participate as equipment suppliers, engineering consultants, or members of bidding consortiums.

The desalination sector has moderate ICV requirements (typically 30%–40%) because substantial equipment components are necessarily imported. Many successful foreign companies partner with Saudi equipment distributors or establish local service and maintenance operations to improve their ICV scores.

### Healthcare, life sciences and digital health

![Digital Healthcare Technology Solutions](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1576091160550-112173782c51?w=800)

The Ministry of Health is executing the largest healthcare modernisation programme in the region. Vision 2030 targets include 24-hour emergency response systems, specialised clinic networks, pharmaceutical manufacturing capacity, biotech research hubs, and comprehensive digital health platforms including telemedicine and AI-driven diagnostics.

Healthcare procurement includes medical devices and equipment, pharmaceutical active ingredients and finished products, healthcare IT systems, construction of medical facilities, and professional staffing and training services. Many of these segments are accessible to foreign companies without local manufacturing.

Medical device companies can participate through distributor networks or direct contracts with the Ministry of Health, government hospitals, and private healthcare providers emerging in the Kingdom. Pharmaceutical companies can secure distribution agreements for innovative drugs, particularly in oncology, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease treatment. Healthcare IT vendors can deploy electronic health records systems, diagnostic imaging software, and telemedicine platforms.

The highest-value opportunities exist in specialised medical services with limited domestic capacity. Diagnostic imaging systems (CT, MRI), robotic surgery platforms, oncology treatment centres, and gene therapy manufacturing represent frontier healthcare sectors where foreign companies enjoy competitive advantage. These often command premium pricing and stronger margins than commoditised services.

Healthcare procurement cycles are longer than construction (24–36 months) but more stable, with predictable annual budgets and multi-year contracts. ICV requirements vary significantly by subsector: medical device distribution might have 20% ICV requirements, while healthcare software implementations might require 40%+ through local staffing, training programmes, and support infrastructure.

### Technology, AI, and data infrastructure

![AI and Data Center Infrastructure](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1551288049-bebda4e38f71?w=800)

NEOM is establishing itself as a global hub for artificial intelligence, data centres, and high-performance computing. The Vision 2030 objective emphasises digital transformation across government agencies, educational institutions, and private sector companies. Combined investment in technology infrastructure exceeds $150 billion through 2030.

NEOM's recent $5 billion DataVolt AI campus partnership (announced February 2025) is the clearest signal of procurement intensity in this sector. The facility, powered by 1.5 GW of renewable energy, targets operational launch by 2028. It requires data centre hardware, AI computing infrastructure, enterprise software, cybersecurity systems, network architecture, and eventually application development services.

Foreign technology companies are effectively pre-qualified for these contracts because Saudi Arabia lacks domestic capacity in advanced semiconductor manufacturing, enterprise data centre hardware, and specialised AI training systems. Companies in the following categories encounter genuine market opportunity: data centre operators and providers, semiconductor and high-performance computing manufacturers, enterprise software vendors (especially ERP, cloud infrastructure, and cybersecurity), artificial intelligence research platforms, and professional services firms specialising in digital transformation.

Technology procurement operates on 18–36 month sales cycles, with significant front-loaded engineering and requirements definition phases. However, once a technology company establishes a foothold in a major Vision 2030 programme, follow-on work and expanded deployments typically follow.

ICV requirements in technology sectors are notably flexible. Because Saudi Arabia must import most advanced computing hardware and software, ICV scoring often emphasises local hiring, training programmes, and research partnerships with Saudi universities rather than domestic hardware manufacturing.

### Tourism and hospitality infrastructure

![Luxury Resort Tourism Development](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1571896349842-c5ba9e50d64d?w=800)

Vision 2030 targets 100 million annual tourist visits by 2030. The programme includes massive investments in tourism infrastructure: The Red Sea Project (luxury coastal resorts), Qiddiya (entertainment and theme parks), AlUla (heritage tourism), NEOM's coastal zones (Sindalah, Magna), and expanded accommodation across pilgrimage destinations for Umrah and Hajj.

Tourism procurement includes hotel construction and operations, leisure and entertainment facilities, tourism software and booking systems, and professional hospitality services. Foreign companies can participate across construction, operations, and technology layers.

Hospitality operations present one of the few Vision 2030 sectors where foreign companies can win management contracts for completed assets. International hotel groups operate the majority of luxury hotels across Saudi Arabia. Hospitality technology companies (property management systems, revenue management software, guest experience platforms) see recurring procurement opportunities.

Tourism infrastructure is characterised by private sector participation, reducing the intensity of ICV scoring compared to government-led infrastructure. However, government-backed projects like The Red Sea Project still maintain ICV requirements of 30%–40%.

### Mining and industrial cities

Vision 2030 emphasises industrial diversification and mining development. The National Industrial Development and Logistics Program (NIDLP) is establishing industrial cities and special economic zones. Phosphate, rare earth, and downstream chemical processing represent specific growth areas.

Mining sector procurement includes exploration and evaluation equipment, mining machinery, processing technology, industrial automation systems, and environmental monitoring infrastructure. Foreign mining companies and equipment suppliers can access procurement through multiple channels: direct supply to Saudi mining companies, joint ventures for mine development, and technology licensing agreements.

Industrial city development creates procurement across materials handling, automation, logistics software, industrial safety systems, and utility infrastructure. These projects operate on longer timescales (3–5 years to full operational status) but generate sustained procurement cycles.

## Accessible versus restricted sectors

Some Vision 2030 sectors are effectively closed to foreign companies without local partnerships. Defence and military industries maintain strategic sector status with restricted foreign ownership and participation. Upstream oil and gas exploration is reserved for Saudi Aramco and designated national operators. Telecommunications infrastructure is regulated with foreign equity caps.

In contrast, renewable energy, healthcare technology, tourism operations, and AI infrastructure welcome foreign participation with relatively transparent procurement processes.

The distinction matters strategically. Foreign companies should focus initial market entry on openly accessible sectors before attempting to penetrate restricted industries through joint ventures or partnerships.

## Procurement timelines and contract structures

![Project Timeline and Procurement Process](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1552664730-d307ca884978?w=800)

Vision 2030 procurement follows several distinct timelines based on project phase and funding source.

Megaproject procurement (NEOM, Red Sea Project) operates on accelerated timelines. Once a project component is greenlit for execution, procurement typically moves from initial requirements definition to contract signature within 12–18 months. This accelerated cycle reflects political delivery pressure and dedicated project management offices. However, the qualification phase for new contractors can take 6–12 months, making total time-to-contract potentially longer for companies new to a specific project.

Government ministry and agency procurement follows standard Saudi Government Tenders and Procurement Law (GTPL) procedures. These typically require 4–6 weeks for tender documentation, 4–8 weeks for bid preparation and submission, 4–8 weeks for evaluation, and 2–4 weeks for contract negotiation. Total time-to-contract is typically 18–24 months from initial capability discussion to signed agreement.

Regulated sector procurement (telecommunications, utilities, financial services) often follows longer timelines, with regulatory approval phases extending total procurement to 24–36 months.

PIF-backed investment vehicles operate with higher autonomy, sometimes compressing procurement cycles to 12–18 months for time-sensitive projects.

Contract structures in Vision 2030 procurement typically follow FIDIC terms for construction and engineering projects, with extensive amendments reflecting the project's specific risk allocation and the Kingdom's preferences. Payment cycles commonly extend to 90–120 days, which is standard for Saudi government projects but requires careful working capital management.

Foreign contractors should expect ICV performance warranties as part of most contracts, with penalties for failure to meet committed ICV improvements. This differs from typical international practice and requires careful structuring during contract negotiation.

## Market entry strategies and competitive positioning

![Strategic Business Partnerships](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1552664730-d307ca884978?w=800)

Foreign companies pursuing Vision 2030 opportunities face three primary market entry pathways.

Direct government contracting requires establishing a registered company in Saudi Arabia with appropriate banking, tax, and regulatory compliance infrastructure. This pathway works well for large multinational companies with existing regional presence, established capability in the relevant sector, and sufficient scale to justify dedicated Saudi operations.

Joint ventures with Saudi partners reduce qualification barriers and automatically improve ICV scores. Successful joint venture partners typically have existing government relationships, established local supply chains, or specific technical capabilities that complement the foreign company's offerings. The trade-off is equity dilution and shared control over commercial decisions.

Subcontracting through established general contractors already active in Vision 2030 projects provides a lower-risk entry pathway. This approach works well for companies with specialised capabilities (advanced materials, diagnostic equipment, software platforms) that integrate into larger project deliverables. However, subcontractors have limited visibility into long-term pipeline and are price-sensitive to general contractors' margin requirements.

Successful foreign companies typically combine pathways: establish a direct presence for marquee customers and strategic capabilities, form joint ventures for large infrastructure programmes, and subcontract specialised capabilities through project partners.

## Managing regulatory requirements and Saudisation

Vision 2030 projects enforce Saudisation requirements mandating that a percentage of workforce be Saudi nationals. In NEOM and other major projects, requirements range from 30% to 50% depending on the job category, with higher percentages for skilled technical roles.

For foreign companies, this translates directly to recruitment and training costs. Companies must budget for HR infrastructure to hire, vet, and onboard Saudi nationals, often requiring visa sponsorship and accommodation. Training programmes for Saudi employees extend project costs but are mandatory for contract compliance.

Saudisation requirements, however, are also leverage points for improving ICV scores. Companies that invest in training programmes, create mentorship arrangements, or develop career progression for Saudi employees demonstrate measurable local value creation. This improves positioning for future contract awards.

## Navigating the IKTVA programme and ICV improvement

Companies winning multiple Vision 2030 contracts should enroll in the IKTVA programme (In-Kingdom Total Value Add), Saudi Aramco's original localisation initiative that has evolved into a broader Kingdom-wide framework.

IKTVA enrollment requires completion of a baseline survey and development of a 5-year improvement action plan. The survey quantifies current ICV across localised goods and services, Saudi workforce compensation, training and development, local supplier development, and research and development investment.

Five-year action plans typically target 50% to 70% ICV improvement through phased local investment. For example, a software company might commit to: opening a Saudi development centre (year 2), establishing a partnership with a Saudi university for research (year 2), hiring 15 Saudi software engineers (year 3), and establishing a local support operations centre (year 4).

Companies that execute against their action plans often see preferential treatment in subsequent government procurement because demonstrated ICV improvement signals commitment to long-term Kingdom investment.

Tourism and hospitality offer lower ICV burden.

## FAQ: What Foreign Companies Actually Need to Know About Vision 2030 Procurement

### What is Vision 2030?

Vision 2030 is Saudi Arabia's long-term economic diversification and development strategy launched in April 2016. The initiative aims to reduce the Kingdom's dependency on oil, diversify the economy, and build a knowledge-based society across three pillars: A Vibrant Society (social, cultural, health development), A Thriving Economy (economic diversification, innovation, private sector growth), and An Ambitious Nation (government transparency, operational efficiency). The programme encompasses 96 strategic objectives across 16 economic sectors and channels over $900 billion through the Public Investment Fund (PIF) and government ministries.

### Can foreign companies participate in Vision 2030 procurement?

Yes. Foreign companies can participate in most Vision 2030 procurement, particularly in sectors where Saudi Arabia lacks domestic capacity or technological expertise. Renewable energy, healthcare technology, desalination, AI and data infrastructure, and tourism operations all actively welcome foreign participation. Some sectors remain restricted or require local partnerships: defence and military industries, upstream oil and gas, and telecommunications maintain government controls or foreign ownership caps. Accessibility depends on the specific sector and project structure.

### Which Vision 2030 sectors are most accessible to foreign companies?

The most accessible sectors are:

Renewable energy and green hydrogen: The Kingdom targets 130 GW by 2030 and lacks domestic manufacturing capacity. Foreign technology providers, EPC contractors, and O&M service providers encounter limited domestic competition.

Healthcare technology and life sciences: Foreign medical device companies, pharmaceutical suppliers, and healthcare IT vendors participate widely. Digital health, diagnostic imaging, and specialised treatment centres represent frontier opportunities.

Desalination and water infrastructure: Advanced reverse osmosis technology, energy recovery systems, and automated monitoring equipment are sourced internationally. EPC contractors participate in plant construction and deployment.

Technology, AI, and data infrastructure: NEOM's $150+ billion technology investment drive creates unmet demand for data centre hardware, AI computing platforms, enterprise software, and cybersecurity systems.

Tourism and hospitality: Foreign hotel operators, hospitality management companies, and tourism software vendors operate across Vision 2030 tourism projects.

Construction materials and equipment: Foreign suppliers of specialised materials, heavy equipment, and project management software participate widely, though typically through local distributors or established contractors.

### What is ICV and how does it affect Vision 2030 contracts?

In-Country Value (ICV) is a scoring mechanism used in government procurement that measures the percentage of economic value a company generates or retains within Saudi Arabia. ICV encompasses five dimensions: localized goods and services sourcing, compensation paid to Saudi nationals, training and development of Saudi employees, development of local suppliers, and local research and development investment.

ICV affects contract awards by acting as a scoring criterion, typically weighted 30%–50% of total evaluation score, alongside price and technical capability. A company with a lower bid price but lower ICV score may lose to a competitor with a higher ICV score even if that competitor's bid price is higher. This is particularly true in government and megaproject procurement.

For foreign companies, ICV directly influences competitiveness. Strategies to improve ICV include establishing local presence, hiring and training Saudi nationals, sourcing from Saudi suppliers, forming joint ventures with Saudi partners, and investing in local research or development partnerships.

### How is ICV score calculated?

The IKTVA (In-Kingdom Total Value Add) formula calculates ICV as:

ICV score = (A + B + C + D + R) / X * (1 + E)

Where:
A = value of localized goods and services sourced
B = compensation paid to Saudi nationals
C = training and development investment in Saudi nationals
D = value of local supplier development spending
R = local research and development investment
X = total in-Kingdom costs
E = export revenue adjustment factor (up to 10% based on export revenue ratio)

Companies that source exclusively from Saudi suppliers, hire all Saudi staff, and reinvest profits locally can theoretically achieve ICV scores of 60%–80%. Foreign-owned or multinational companies typically achieve 15%–40% ICV initially, and improve through deliberate localisation investments.

### What procurement timelines should foreign companies expect?

Procurement timelines vary by project type:

Megaproject procurement (NEOM, Red Sea Project): 12–18 months from requirements definition to contract signature, with 6–12 month contractor qualification phase for new entrants.

Government ministry procurement: 18–24 months typically, following Saudi Government Tenders and Procurement Law (GTPL) procedures.

Regulated sector procurement: 24–36 months, including regulatory approval phases.

Immediate contractor timelines can be faster: companies already pre-qualified with a project can move from new requirement identification to contract within 4–6 months.

### Do foreign companies need a local partner?

Not universally, but practically, most do. Large multinational companies with existing Saudi subsidiaries and established capability can pursue direct government contracting. However, a local joint venture partner offers significant advantages: improved ICV scoring, existing government relationships, established supply chains, and regulatory navigation support.

Successful models include: 50/50 joint ventures with Saudi industrial holding companies, 60/40 partnerships where foreign companies maintain operational control, and subcontracting arrangements through established general contractors.

### What is Saudisation and how does it affect project costs?

Saudisation requires that a specified percentage of project workforce be Saudi nationals. In NEOM and other major projects, requirements typically range from 30%–50% depending on job category, with higher percentages for skilled technical roles.

For foreign companies, Saudisation increases recruitment and training costs, HR infrastructure costs, and accommodation expenses. However, it also creates leverage for improving ICV scores, as demonstrated local workforce development generates measurable value-add.

### How should foreign companies approach the IKTVA programme?

Companies winning multiple Vision 2030 contracts should enroll in the IKTVA programme through Saudi Aramco (now Kingdom-wide). The process involves:

1. Baseline survey: Quantifying current ICV across five dimensions.
2. Five-year action plan: Committing to specific localisation investments (local hiring, supplier development, research partnerships, training programmes).
3. Annual progress reporting: Demonstrating execution against the plan.
4. Continuous improvement: Building evidence of measurable ICV improvement, which improves positioning for future government procurement.

Companies with credible, executed ICV improvement plans often receive preferential treatment in subsequent procurement because they demonstrate strategic commitment to Kingdom investment.

### What are the biggest mistakes foreign companies make in Vision 2030 procurement?

Common mistakes include:

Underestimating ICV importance: Treating ICV as a compliance checkbox rather than a competitive advantage. Companies that invest intentionally in ICV improvement win more contracts.

Ignoring sector accessibility: Pursuing opportunities in restricted sectors (defence, upstream oil) without understanding ownership restrictions or partnership requirements.

Failing to qualify early: Waiting until specific opportunities emerge to begin contractor qualification processes. Successful companies invest 6–12 months in qualification before bidding.

Structuring without local knowledge: Assuming standard international contract terms will be accepted. NEOM and other projects use heavily amended FIDIC terms with extensive risk reallocations.

Underinvesting in government relationships: Vision 2030 procurement requires sustained relationship investment across multiple government entities, project offices, and regulatory bodies. Companies that rely on competitive tendering alone without relationship building face higher costs and longer sales cycles.

Viewing Saudi Arabia as a short-term market: Companies that succeed in Vision 2030 typically commit to 5–10 year Kingdom presence, not project-by-project engagements.

### What resources should foreign companies use to monitor Vision 2030 opportunities?

Primary resources include:

NEOM's official procurement portal and contractor registry for megaproject opportunities.

Tender websites operated by Saudi government entities and municipalities.

PIF investment announcements for major funding approvals.

Sector-specific industry associations and chambers of commerce in Saudi Arabia.

Direct engagement with government ministry procurement teams (Ministry of Health, Ministry of Energy, Ministry of Transport).

Secondary resources include industry publications covering Vision 2030 development, consultant reports from strategy firms focused on the region, and peer company case studies from companies with established Saudi operations.
