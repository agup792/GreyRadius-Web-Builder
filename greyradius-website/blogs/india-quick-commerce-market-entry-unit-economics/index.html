India quick commerce entry – when fast delivery creates growth and when it destroys margin

5 November 2026 · GreyRadius Consulting

A shopper who runs out of milk before breakfast values a delivery that arrives in minutes. A shopper buying a month’s worth of detergent may care more about price per wash. Both purchases can happen on a quick commerce app, but the reason to buy, the pack that sells, and the margin the brand can retain are different.

A quick commerce market entry in India should begin with the purchase occasion. What does the customer need right now? Which pack solves that need? Can the brand keep it available locally and make money after platform deductions, advertising, and inventory losses?

For FMCG brands, the case for expansion rests on three things. The product fits a recurring or urgent purchase occasion, the brand earns a viable contribution under normal trading conditions, and the supply team can keep that product in stock across the locations it serves.

1. Start with the purchase occasion, then choose the pack

Products tend to fit quick commerce when immediate availability matters. A forgotten cooking ingredient, a snack for guests, a household essential that has run out, or a personal care product needed that evening can all serve a clear purpose. The same product may need a different pack for a weekly shop or a monthly stock-up.

Product factorWhat to testWhere margin can erode
Purchase frequencyHow often does the need recur, and do shoppers reorder at the regular price?Introductory offers generate first purchases, but few shoppers return.
Pack sizeDoes the pack suit a top-up, an immediate-use occasion, or a planned stock-up?A low-ticket pack sells quickly but leaves too little contribution after per-unit costs. A larger pack ties up stock if it moves slowly.
Shelf lifeHow much usable shelf life remains after production, transport, and receipt at the stocking location?Slow-moving inventory is marked down, rejected, returned, or written off.
Price pointWill shoppers pay the regular price for the product and the convenience of getting it quickly?A discount masks weak willingness to pay, or a cheaper pack shifts sales from a more profitable channel.
Replenishment needsCan the brand refill high-demand locations at a viable order size and delivery frequency?Small, frequent deliveries and emergency transfers consume the contribution from higher sales.

A single-serve beverage may fit an immediate consumption occasion, while a small multipack may leave more contribution per sale. A chilled product may have strong repeat demand, but its viability depends on how quickly it sells at each location, how reliably it can be replenished, and who absorbs losses on unsold stock. Those questions are easier to answer for a handful of SKUs than for an entire catalogue.

2. Build the unit economics from the settlement statement

Quick commerce unit economics vary by commercial arrangement. A platform or distributor may buy the goods at an agreed price, or the brand may sell through a marketplace arrangement with deductions from sales. Start with the revenue the brand actually earns under its agreement, excluding GST. Then reconcile that figure against invoices, debit notes, and settlements.

Commercial factorWhat the brand should verify
Platform and distributor termsSupply price or commission, applicable handling and fulfilment charges, payment timing, deductions, and who owns the stock at each stage.
PromotionsWho funds each offer, how the contribution is settled, and how volumes change once the offer ends.
AdvertisingWhat the brand spends on sponsored placements and campaigns, how much demand that spending generates, and what happens when it is reduced.
Returns and inventory lossesWho pays for damaged, rejected, short-dated, and unsold stock. Check how credits, recoveries, and write-offs appear in the settlement.
Replenishment and working capitalWhat it costs to serve each city, how much stock must be held locally, and how long cash stays tied up between dispatch and settlement.

Calculate contribution by SKU and city. Start with the brand’s revenue and deduct product cost, applicable channel charges, brand-funded promotions, attributable advertising, replenishment costs, and expected inventory losses. If a cost has already been deducted from revenue, count it once. Track settlement delays and inventory holding separately to understand how much cash expansion would require.

What a positive margin can hide

Suppose a brand recognises ₹80 in revenue per unit, excluding GST, before the deductions shown below. These figures are illustrative.

Per-unit economicsAmount
Brand revenue before the deductions below₹80
Product cost−₹40
Channel and handling charges−₹8
Brand-funded promotions−₹10
Attributable advertising−₹9
Replenishment and expected inventory losses−₹7
Contribution before fixed costs₹6

An additional ₹7 of promotional funding would turn the ₹6 contribution into a ₹1 loss per unit if the other costs stayed constant. A promotion can still be worth running if it produces enough incremental sales and profitable repeat purchases. Test that by tracking what customers buy after the offer ends and whether total contribution rises once campaign costs are included.

Also check whether quick commerce creates sales the brand would otherwise miss. If shoppers move an existing purchase from modern trade to an app, the brand may report channel growth while earning less on the same demand. Compare total sales and contribution across channels in pilot locations and, where possible, against similar locations outside the pilot.

3. Give quick commerce a clear role alongside other channels

For an online grocery market entry in India, the same brand can use different packs to serve different shopping missions. The choice should reflect how people buy the product and what it costs the brand to reach them.

ChannelWhere it can work wellWhat to measure
Quick commerceImmediate consumption, forgotten items, and top-ups where speed affects the purchase decision.Local availability, regular-price sell-through, contribution after promotions and ads, and stock losses.
Modern tradePlanned grocery shops, physical discovery, in-store sampling, and larger packs.Sales per store, trade spend, shelf visibility, and retailer replenishment.
Conventional e-commerceSearch-led discovery, products that benefit from detailed comparison, and bundles shoppers can wait for.Customer acquisition cost, fulfilment cost, returns, and repeat purchasing.

Consider a new breakfast brand. A trial pack could work on quick commerce in neighbourhoods where shoppers already buy similar products for immediate use. Modern trade could help shoppers sample the product. Conventional e-commerce could carry a larger bundle for buyers who already know they like it. Each channel earns its place by serving a distinct purchase occasion at a viable cost.

4. Run a limited city pilot before widening distribution

A practical quick commerce brand strategy in India starts with a few SKUs and stocking locations the brand can serve reliably. Choose cities based on evidence of category demand, platform coverage in the target neighbourhoods, and the cost of replenishing those locations. If possible, pair a city where the brand already has demand with a second city where awareness or supply conditions differ. That helps show how much the result depends on local conditions.

Define the test before dispatching stock. Select a core SKU, a pack suited to the immediate-use occasion, and a higher-value pack if there is a clear reason to test one. Agree on opening stock, replenishment cadence, expiry handling, visibility spend, and settlement terms. Set thresholds for contribution, availability, and inventory losses before launch.

Separate launch demand from regular-price demand. Capture a baseline, record the effect of any launch promotion, and keep the pilot running long enough to observe a normal replenishment cycle and a realistic repurchase window for the category. Where customer-level data is unavailable, use regular-price sales over time and disclose that individual repeat purchases could not be verified.

Review performance below the city average. A few high-volume locations can conceal stockouts in one neighbourhood and excess inventory in another. Review the results by SKU and stocking location wherever the platform shares that data.

Pilot measureWhat it tells you
Units sold per location while in stockWhether shoppers buy when they can find the product, with less distortion from stockouts.
Regular-price sales and repeat purchases where measurableWhether demand holds after introductory discounts end.
Contribution per unit and total contributionWhether volume generates money after actual deductions, advertising, and inventory losses.
Availability, stock age, and write-offsWhether replenishment can support demand without building excess inventory.
Cross-channel sales and contributionWhether app sales appear to add demand or shift existing purchases.
Cash tied up in inventory and settlementsHow much working capital the rollout will require as locations increase.

Expand when regular-price demand holds, contribution clears the brand’s threshold, and the supply team can maintain availability with acceptable losses. If demand is strong but contribution falls short, test a specific change such as pack size, promotion depth, or replenishment frequency. If sales depend on persistent discounts or stock repeatedly ages out, pause that SKU or location before committing more inventory.

Frequently asked questions

Which products are suitable for quick commerce in India?

Products that solve a frequent, urgent, or immediate-use need are good candidates. Examples include staple top-ups, snacks, beverages, forgotten cooking ingredients, and household or personal care essentials. Suitability depends on the pack, regular selling price, local sales velocity, and usable shelf life. A category can perform well while individual SKUs lose money.

How should brands evaluate quick commerce profitability?

Use actual settlements to calculate contribution by SKU and city. Account for product cost, platform and distributor deductions, promotions, advertising, replenishment, returns, and write-offs. Then assess regular-price demand, working capital requirements, and the effect on sales and contribution in other channels.

Should a new brand launch on quick commerce first?

It can when shoppers already understand the category, the product solves an immediate need, and the brand can support local stock and visibility. If shoppers need demonstrations, education, or physical sampling, modern trade or conventional e-commerce may offer a better starting point. A limited quick commerce pilot can test whether there is enough demand to justify a broader launch.

What should a city-level pilot measure?

Track sell-through while in stock, regular-price demand, repeat purchases where measurable, contribution after actual costs, stock age, returns, write-offs, and replenishment reliability. Compare results across locations and check for changes in other channels. Use that evidence to decide which SKUs to scale, which to adjust, and which to withdraw.

Before committing to broader FMCG quick commerce distribution, ask which SKU the brand would still choose to sell in each city at the regular price, with the expected advertising bill and the stock losses observed during the pilot. That answer gives the commercial team a concrete starting point for the rollout.
