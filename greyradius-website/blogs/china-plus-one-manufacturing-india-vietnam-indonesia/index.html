China-Plus-One Manufacturing: How to Compare India, Vietnam, and Indonesia Beyond Labour Costs

Why the right manufacturing location depends less on wage rates and more on supplier ecosystems, landed costs, operational resilience, and long-term scalability.

12 November 2026 · GreyRadius Consulting

Introduction: The Cheapest Factory May Be the Most Expensive Decision

For manufacturers looking to diversify production beyond China, India, Vietnam, and Indonesia frequently emerge as potential alternatives. Each offers a different combination of industrial capabilities, labour availability, market access, government incentives, and growth opportunities.

Yet many manufacturing location decisions begin with a misleading comparison: labour costs per worker. A country with lower wages may appear more competitive on paper. But if the factory must import most components, maintain additional inventory, absorb unpredictable delivery schedules, or invest heavily in workforce training, the apparent savings can disappear before the finished product reaches the customer.

Consider two hypothetical manufacturing locations. Factory A offers labour costs 25% below Factory B, but depends on imported components, requires longer inventory buffers, and faces higher inland transportation costs. Factory B has higher wages but benefits from established local suppliers, shorter production cycles, and more predictable export logistics. Which is more competitive? The answer depends on the total cost of producing, financing, and delivering the product–not simply wages.

China-plus-one is not necessarily about replacing China. For many manufacturers, it means retaining critical production or sourcing capabilities in China while establishing an additional base to reduce concentration risk, improve customer access, or create a more competitive supply chain. The strategic question is: Which location can deliver the required product, at the required quality and volume, to intended customers, with the most competitive risk-adjusted economics?

1. Why China-Plus-One Has Become a Strategic Manufacturing Decision

China has developed extensive manufacturing ecosystems combining scale, specialised suppliers, engineering capabilities, industrial infrastructure, and export logistics. A component supplier may be located within the same industrial cluster; tooling, packaging, testing, maintenance, and equipment providers may be nearby. This concentration reduces procurement complexity, production lead times, and working capital requirements.

Relocating a factory without relocating or replacing the supplier ecosystem can erode these advantages. A successful supply chain diversification strategy must distinguish three objectives: reducing concentration risk; improving commercial competitiveness through trade access or delivered costs; and creating future growth options through new customers, regional demand, and adjacent products.

These objectives do not always point to the same country. A manufacturer serving European customers may prioritise export logistics and trade eligibility; one targeting Indian automotive manufacturers may prioritise customer proximity and local suppliers; a battery-materials producer may evaluate Indonesia for relevant upstream inputs and processing infrastructure.

2. India vs Vietnam vs Indonesia: Three Different Manufacturing Propositions

India: Domestic market scale and industrial breadth

India offers a large domestic market, an extensive workforce, and established clusters across automotive, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, engineering, and selected electronics segments. Its proposition is particularly strong when manufacturing serves both domestic demand and exports. Outcomes differ substantially across states and industrial corridors.

Vietnam: Export-oriented manufacturing integration

Vietnam has developed strong export ecosystems in electronics assembly, garments, footwear, and selected consumer products. Established international manufacturing networks can support overseas customers, although upstream input dependence remains important for many product categories.

Indonesia: Resource-linked industries and regional demand

Indonesia combines a substantial domestic market with opportunities in automotive, consumer products, metals processing, and resource-linked manufacturing. Its advantages can be compelling when raw material access or local customers outweigh additional logistics complexity.

3. Supplier Depth: The Most Overlooked Manufacturing Location Variable

Factories depend on networks of raw-material providers, component makers, tooling companies, maintenance specialists, packaging suppliers, logistics operators, and testing facilities. The presence of suppliers is not equivalent to technical capability. An automotive manufacturer needing precision-machined parts and documented quality systems cannot assume that general metalworking capacity is adequate.

Supplier assessment should cover the number of technically qualified suppliers, capacity for ramp-up, quality certifications, imported-material dependence, alternatives for critical components, and financial and delivery performance. Distinguish suppliers that exist, suppliers that are capable, and suppliers ready to contract.

India has depth in several automotive, pharmaceutical, chemical, and engineering clusters, but not uniformly across regions. Vietnam has export-oriented ecosystems while some specialised inputs remain imported. Indonesia has advantages in selected metals, automotive, consumer, and resource-processing industries. Map suppliers against the actual bill of materials rather than a national ranking.

GreyRadius perspective: A factory’s competitiveness depends not only on what it manufactures internally, but also on what its surrounding industrial ecosystem can reliably deliver.

4. Import Dependence: When Lower Wages Create Higher Working Capital

A lower-cost assembly base may still import machinery, components, chemicals, electronics, or specialised materials from China. Imports introduce freight, insurance, customs processing, duties, currency exposure, and longer replenishment cycles. These can require safety stock and create production interruptions.

Imagine imported components account for 60% of material spend. Relocating final assembly may diversify geography without diversifying upstream supply. Measure both local value addition and critical supplier concentration. A single imported component can stop a line even when most manufacturing value is local.

5. Logistics: The Difference Between Factory Cost and Delivered Cost

Compare complete inbound and outbound routes, not just distance to a port. Inbound logistics moves materials, components, and equipment into the factory; outbound logistics moves finished products to customers. Evaluate transport costs, customs clearance, shipment frequency, congestion, transit variability, and alternative routes.

India’s performance varies by industrial corridor, state, customer location, and port or rail connectivity. Vietnam’s northern and southern export ecosystems have different supplier and gateway advantages. Indonesia’s archipelagic geography makes inter-island handling, site location, and customer proximity especially important. Logistics must be modelled route by route.

6. Power Reliability: A Cost That Rarely Appears in Wage Comparisons

For continuous-process industries and sensitive equipment, voltage stability, outages, and contracted capacity can matter as much as tariffs. Interruptions can create lost output, scrap, damaged equipment, and maintenance expense. Assess effective tariffs, grid performance, backup generation, renewable access, and lead times for new power connections at the industrial-zone and individual-site levels.

7. Workforce Capabilities: Labour Productivity Matters More Than Labour Rates

Low monthly wages do not necessarily mean low cost per accepted unit. Compare staffing per line, training duration, yield, supervision, turnover, technical skills, and production ramp-up. Labour cost per good unit = total direct labour cost ÷ accepted production units.

India has a large technical and engineering pool, Vietnam has experienced export-manufacturing workers, and Indonesia has a substantial workforce with regionally varied industrial specialisation. Local hiring evidence and practical skills assessments matter more than country-wide wage averages.

8. Customer Proximity: Manufacturing Should Follow Commercial Strategy

Factory location is also a commercial choice. Customer proximity affects lead times, service, product customisation, inventory, qualification, and sometimes local-content requirements. Ask where current demand is concentrated, where credible future demand will emerge, and whether local production changes customer eligibility or purchasing decisions. Support assumed benefits with customer evidence rather than speculation.

9. Expansion Options: Choosing a Location for the Next Ten Years

A location suitable for the first production line may be unable to support the next three. Test adjacent land, zoning, utility capacity, wastewater permissions, warehouse space, labour supply, and access to new suppliers and customers. The feasibility model should include a credible expansion case, not just initial capacity.

10. Country Screening Is Not the Same as Industrial-Zone and Site Selection

Level 1 – Country screening

Screen trade access, macroeconomic conditions, demand, policy, industrial capabilities, and investment rules. Exclude countries that fail non-negotiable requirements before detailed diligence.

Level 2 – Industrial cluster and zone

Evaluate supplier proximity, workforce pools, infrastructure, logistics corridors, utilities, and incentives. In India compare relevant state clusters; in Vietnam compare northern and southern ecosystems; in Indonesia assess specific industrial corridors and islands. Validate zone claims with suppliers, logistics quotes, and existing tenants.

Level 3 – Individual site assessment

Verify land title or lease, permits, water and wastewater, grid connection, truck access, construction requirements, environmental compliance, and expansion land. Planned infrastructure is not equivalent to committed service availability.

Decision rule: Select the country for strategic fit, the industrial cluster for ecosystem strength, and the site for operational feasibility.

11. Landed Cost: The Financial Model That Should Drive Location Selection

Landed cost must capture the cost of delivering a compliant, saleable product to the customer. Include materials and inbound freight; labour and training; utilities and overhead; outbound logistics; duties and non-recoverable taxes; quality losses; inventory financing; capital recovery; and expected disruption costs. Do not double-count material cost in inventory financing or treat recoverable indirect taxes as permanent expenses.

12. Working Capital: The Hidden Cost of Diversification

A new plant may need longer raw-material replenishment, higher safety stock, customer qualification inventory, and more finished goods. Cash conversion cycle = days inventory outstanding + days sales outstanding − days payable outstanding.

Illustration: At US$30 million in annual cash operating expenditure, an additional 20 days of inventory represents approximately US$1.64 million of incremental investment (US$30m ÷ 365 × 20). At 10% financing cost, that implies about US$164,000 annually before warehousing, insurance, and obsolescence. A detailed model should use the inventory-specific cost base.

13. Disruption Scenarios: Testing Whether Diversification Actually Reduces Risk

Two factories in different countries may still depend on the same critical Chinese supplier or shipping corridor. Test correlated risk and the ability to recover, not simply geographic separation.

Scenario 1: Base-case operations

Model planned volume, normal supplier lead times, standard logistics performance, and expected productivity.

Scenario 2: Supply chain disruption

Stress critical-component delays, freight increases, customs bottlenecks, and route closure. Estimate safety-stock coverage, alternatives, lost contribution margin, and customer penalties.

Scenario 3: Operational ramp-up stress

Stress low yield, slow hiring, constrained utilities, and delayed customer approval. Evaluate cash burn, breakeven timing, and contingency funding.

14. Manufacturing Location Selection: A Five-Stage Decision Framework

Stage 1 – Define requirements

Document product specifications, technical standards, volumes, customers, destinations, and the objectives of diversification.

Stage 2 – Screen countries

Apply strategic criteria and hard exclusions for legal feasibility, infrastructure, compliance, and critical materials.

Stage 3 – Map ecosystems

Shortlist specific clusters and zones; validate suppliers, logistics, utilities, and workforce with independent evidence.

Stage 4 – Model economics and risk

Compare consistent landed-cost and cash-flow cases, capital expenditure, ramp-up, incentives, working capital, NPV, IRR, and disruptions.

Stage 5 – Validate the site

Confirm land, permits, utility commitments, suppliers, labour, and routes before capital commitment.

16. Common Mistakes That Undermine China-Plus-One Investments

Common errors include following competitor investments without matching their requirements; mistaking announced investment for qualified supplier capacity; comparing factory-gate costs rather than landed cost; overvaluing incentives; neglecting expansion; and assuming a second assembly location eliminates dependence on common upstream suppliers. China-plus-one is a supply chain architecture decision, not simply a real estate or wage decision.

17. What a Successful China-Plus-One Strategy Should Deliver

Management should emerge with a defendable investment case: delivered costs by destination, capital and working-capital requirements, qualified supplier availability, commercial production timing, customer access, expansion options, and tested downside scenarios. The strongest manufacturing network balances competitiveness with flexibility and resilience.

Frequently Asked Questions

How should manufacturers choose a China-plus-one location?

Define product and customer requirements, screen countries, compare industrial clusters, then validate individual sites using landed-cost, working-capital, and disruption models.

Is India or Vietnam better for manufacturing?

Neither is universally better. India may suit domestic-market access and certain engineering ecosystems; Vietnam may suit established export assembly clusters. Compare actual product requirements and delivered costs.

What costs are missed in factory location decisions?

Imported-input freight, customs, safety stock, financing, quality losses, ramp-up, backup power, training, maintenance, inland logistics, and delayed customer qualification.

How do supplier ecosystems affect site selection?

They determine qualified input availability, production reliability, inventory requirements, lead times, and alternative sourcing. Evaluate at component and cluster levels.

Is Indonesia a good location for manufacturing investment?

It can be, especially for selected resource-linked, automotive, and domestic-market sectors. Site-specific logistics, infrastructure, and supplier readiness are decisive.

How can companies reduce China dependence without moving all production?

Diversify critical suppliers, shift selected lines, establish secondary assembly, and build alternative inventory and distribution arrangements in phases.

Conclusion: The Right Manufacturing Location Is a System, Not a Country

India, Vietnam, and Indonesia offer credible China-plus-one opportunities, but the decision cannot be reduced to wages, incentives, or headline growth. Supplier depth affects imports; imports affect inventory; inventory affects working capital; logistics affects customer service; and skills affect productivity and quality. The best location delivers the strongest combination of cost competitiveness, resilience, customer access, and long-term execution capability.
