A Sales Spike Can Hide a Weak City

How GreyRadius helped a consumer brand use a city-level pilot to decide which products to scale on India’s quick-commerce platforms.

5 November 2026 · GreyRadius Consulting

Context

A consumer brand preparing to enter India’s quick-commerce channel planned to launch a limited assortment before expanding. The first decision was how much inventory and promotional spend to commit. The larger decision was what the brand would need to see before backing more products or entering another city.

A strong launch week could make that second decision look easy. A promotion could lift orders while reducing what the brand earned on each sale. If the team used that spike to set its next stock commitment, it could end up with excess inventory once the offer ended. The pilot had to give leadership a basis for committing more capital under ordinary trading conditions.

The Core Challenge

The surface problem was securing a platform listing and choosing an opening assortment. The structural issue was that the conditions for scale sat at the level of each SKU in each city. A listing gave the brand access to shoppers. The case for expansion depended on whether those shoppers bought the product, whether the brand could keep it available, and whether the resulting orders contributed enough to justify more stock.

The teams involved could read the same sales result differently. Commercial could see a fast-selling SKU and want to widen distribution. Supply could see a promotional peak that was difficult to replenish. Finance could see the same orders yield little contribution after platform charges and discounts. A stockout complicated the picture further. Low sales during a stockout gave the team little basis for judging underlying demand.

The Decision Shift

The unit of expansion became the SKU in a city. Platform access opened the channel. Each product then had to earn a larger stock commitment through demonstrated demand, dependable replenishment, and positive contribution after platform fees, promotions, and returns.

That changed the question leadership asked after a strong sales week. How much of the volume depended on the offer? Could the brand keep the product in stock at that pace? What did it earn on each order after the costs it carried? Reading those answers together gave the team three distinct choices. Expand products that cleared all three tests, revise products with a specific issue to resolve, and drop those with a weak commercial case.

What Changed in Execution

The opening assortment favoured products shoppers were likely to want immediately, at pack sizes and price points suited to the channel. City selection weighed target buyer density and platform coverage alongside the brand’s ability to replenish stock. An attractive market still needed a supply setup capable of keeping the opening assortment available.

Platform terms clarified which fees the brand would pay, who would fund promotions, and who would bear returns and inventory losses. Those terms mattered directly to the scale decision. The same sales volume could support a different stock commitment depending on how much of the discount or inventory risk fell to the brand.

SKU-level sell-through guided replenishment priorities. In-stock rates gave the team context for interpreting sales, while contribution after promotions informed whether to expand, revise, or drop a product. Commercial, supply, and finance could therefore review the same SKU against a common set of facts before committing more inventory or promotional spend.

Business Impact Delivered

The pilot gave leadership a basis for deciding which products merited more inventory and promotional spend. It also made the next decision more specific. A SKU with demand and unreliable supply called for a replenishment fix before a wider rollout. A SKU that sold quickly on promotion and produced weak contribution called for a review of the price, pack, or promotional terms before another spend commitment.

Actual margins and subsequent expansion outcomes were undisclosed. The demonstrated impact was a clearer basis for allocating stock and spend at SKU level.

Key Outcomes

The pilot tracked three measures relevant to the scale decision. Pilot SKU count defined the scope of the test. In-stock rate by SKU showed how consistently shoppers could buy each product. Contribution per order after platform fees, promotions, and returns showed what each sale earned after those costs. Actual values were undisclosed.

In quick commerce, sales velocity earns a larger inventory commitment when the brand can sustain availability and retain contribution at that pace.
