Clinical Interest Was Not a Purchase Pathway.

How GreyRadius helped an international medical device company align clinical acceptance, purchasing authority, and service readiness for entry into Indian hospitals.

30 October 2026 · GreyRadius Consulting

Context

For an international medical device company entering India, getting a hospital to evaluate a product was only the beginning. The commercial test was whether that evaluation could progress to an approved purchase and a supported deployment.

A clinician could see value while procurement lacked a viable commercial proposal. A distributor could secure access while installation or maintenance remained uncertain. Each stakeholder could support the product without the hospital being ready to adopt it.

The market-entry risk sat between these decisions. More hospital access could generate more evaluations while leaving the route to revenue unresolved.

The Core Challenge

The surface problem was distribution: finding partners capable of opening hospital doors and securing clinical attention.

The structural issue was fragmented ownership across the adoption pathway. Clinical acceptance, purchasing authority, budget availability, and service readiness belonged to different stakeholders. Progress with one did not establish commitment from the others.

An evaluation could therefore appear commercially promising without an identified purchase owner, acceptable economics, or dependable local support. The visible milestone was product assessment; the unresolved execution dependency was whether the hospital could actually buy and deploy.

This distinction mattered for scale. Expanding distributor coverage before resolving those dependencies risked multiplying stalled opportunities. Evaluation volume could grow faster than the company’s ability to distinguish genuine purchase potential from clinical interest.

The constraint was operating maturity: a defined pathway connecting a successful evaluation to an executable purchasing decision.

The Decision Shift

The company shifted from securing distribution to earning hospital adoption.

The strategic realization was that hospital access had commercial value only when clinical acceptance, commercial viability, and service readiness could advance together. These became joint conditions for market entry.

That reframed both account selection and partner selection. A hospital’s willingness to evaluate was insufficient on its own. The opportunity also needed a credible purchasing route and the capacity to support deployment. Likewise, a distributor’s relationships mattered alongside its ability to install, train, maintain, and service.

The governing question changed from “Can this partner get the product into the hospital?” to “Can this hospital move from evaluation to a funded, supported purchase?”

This created a more demanding definition of opportunity quality—and a clearer basis for deciding where commercial effort belonged.

What Changed in Execution

Hospital targeting brought clinical fit, purchasing capacity, and deployment readiness into the same decision. Commercial attention could be directed toward accounts where those conditions aligned, with unresolved dependencies made explicit.

Adoption gates linked clinical champions and evaluators with procurement and budget owners. Clinical enthusiasm no longer stood alone as evidence of purchase readiness. Each opportunity required clarity about who assessed value, who controlled expenditure, and what conditions would permit progression.

Distributor suitability included installation, training, maintenance, and service coverage. Local support became part of the entry decision, reducing the risk that commercial commitments would run ahead of delivery capability.

Pricing was tied to hospital economics. Evaluations were linked to defined purchase criteria, giving clinical and commercial stakeholders a shared basis for judging whether an assessment should lead to an order.

Together, these changes established a common decision structure across accounts. Coordination could focus on the specific condition preventing adoption, giving leadership a clearer view of where intervention was needed.

Business Impact Delivered

The documented change was a clearer adoption pathway, with explicit connections between evaluation, purchasing authority, economics, and deployment support.

The intended commercial benefit was stronger evaluation-to-order conversion and fewer deployments blocked by pricing or service gaps. Earlier visibility into these constraints was also intended to improve resource allocation and make opportunity progression more predictable.

These benefits remain unverified. The available information does not establish faster purchasing, higher conversion, or completed deployments. The case supports a change in operating discipline; it does not yet demonstrate a measured revenue outcome.

Key Outcomes

  • Hospital priorities reflected clinical, purchasing, and deployment conditions.
  • Adoption gates connected clinical evaluation with commercial authority.
  • Distributor criteria included delivery and ongoing service obligations.
  • Evaluations carried defined purchase criteria and hospital-relevant economics.
  • Conversion, cycle-time, purchase, and deployment figures were not provided.

Hospital expansion becomes commercially scalable when each evaluation has a credible route to a funded purchase and a supported deployment. Without that connection, wider access can scale unresolved decisions faster than revenue.
